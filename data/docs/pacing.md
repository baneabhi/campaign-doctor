# Pacing

Educational summary written for the Campaign Doctor demo. It is not vendor documentation.

## What pacing means

Pacing describes whether a campaign is spending its budget at the rate the flight calls for. The pacing ratio is actual spend to date divided by expected spend to date. Expected spend assumes an even rate: budget multiplied by days elapsed, divided by total flight days. A ratio of 1.00 means exactly on plan.

## Pacing thresholds used here

In this workspace a ratio below 0.90 is under-pacing, a ratio from 0.90 to 1.10 is on track, and a ratio above 1.10 is over-pacing. These are working thresholds for triage, not industry standards. A campaign near a threshold deserves a look at its daily trend, because a single ratio hides whether delivery is steady, falling or spiking.

## Under-pacing

Under-pacing means the campaign is not finding enough impressions to buy. The usual causes are a low win rate because bids sit below the clearing price, a small or over-filtered audience that produces few bid opportunities, frequency caps that stop repeat delivery, high price floors, and creatives that stopped serving. To tell them apart, compare bid volume, win rate and effective CPM against the max bid. See troubleshooting-underdelivery.md.

## Over-pacing

Over-pacing means the campaign is spending faster than planned. Common causes are accelerated (as fast as possible) pacing settings, a bid raised mid-flight, a burst of cheap inventory early in the flight, and a budget that was set too small for the flight length. The risk is running out of budget before the flight ends, leaving the last days with no delivery. Fixes include switching to even pacing, adding a daily cap, or lowering the max bid.

## Even versus accelerated pacing

Even pacing spreads the budget across the flight so that each day gets roughly its share. Accelerated pacing spends as quickly as inventory is available, which front-loads delivery. Accelerated suits time-boxed promotions; even suits always-on prospecting.
